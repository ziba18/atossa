# What makes a patient-generated gynaecological symptom summary useful to a GP (2025–2026)

Researcher notes for Atossa ("For my doctor" tab + PDF export). UK/GP-centric; US differences noted.
Method note: NICE pages (NG73, NG88) were read directly from nice.org.uk (full recommendation text). NICE CKS pages returned 403 and could not be read. The ACOG 2026 guideline press release returned 402; ACOG content below comes from search-result snippets only.

## 1. What GPs need in a 10-minute appointment, and what the guidance says about symptom diaries

### Takeaway
UK guidance tells GPs what to take a history of: for suspected endometriosis, a fixed list of symptom patterns plus family history. For heavy bleeding, the nature of the bleeding, related symptoms and impact on quality of life. NICE NG73 tells clinicians to say that a pain and symptom diary "can aid discussions". A summary that fills in those history items directly, and briefly, lines up with what the GP is already meant to ask.

### Cited Findings
- NICE NG73 1.3.1 (endometriosis, last updated Nov 2024): "Suspect endometriosis in women (including young women aged 17 and under) presenting with 1 or more of: chronic pelvic pain; period-related pain (dysmenorrhoea) affecting daily activities and quality of life; deep pain during or after sexual intercourse; period-related or cyclical gastrointestinal symptoms, in particular, painful bowel movements; period-related or cyclical urinary symptoms, in particular, blood in the urine or pain passing urine; infertility in association with 1 or more of the above." — [NICE NG73 Recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)
- NG73 1.3.2 [2024]: "Ask if any first-degree relatives have a history of endometriosis" — [NICE NG73](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)
- NG73 1.3.3 [2017]: "Inform women with suspected or confirmed endometriosis that keeping a pain and symptom diary can aid discussions." — [NICE NG73](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)
- NG73 1.3.4 [2024]: "every person's experience of pain is unique to them and may be expressed in different ways, both verbally and non-verbally", and may vary with cultural background, socioeconomic status and neurodivergence. — [NICE NG73](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)
- NG73 1.1.2: services should have "processes in place for prompt diagnosis … because delays can affect quality of life". NG73 1.5.1–1.5.2 [2024]: run investigations and referral in parallel with first treatment. Offer transvaginal ultrasound to everyone with suspected endometriosis "even if pelvic or abdominal examination is normal", organised by the general practice. — [NICE NG73](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)
- NICE NG88 1.1.1 (heavy menstrual bleeding): "Recognise that heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) has a major impact on a woman's quality of life, and ensure that any intervention aims to improve this rather than focusing on blood loss." — [NICE NG88 Recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Recommendations)
- NG88 1.2.1: take a history that covers "the nature of the bleeding; related symptoms, such as persistent intermenstrual bleeding, pelvic pain and/or pressure symptoms …; impact on her quality of life; other factors that may affect treatment options (such as comorbidities or previous treatment for HMB)." — [NICE NG88](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Recommendations)
- NG88 1.2.4: if HMB comes with other related symptoms, offer a physical examination. 1.2.7: consider coagulation testing if HMB "since their periods started" plus personal or family history suggesting a coagulation disorder. — [NICE NG88](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Recommendations)
- NHS lay criteria for heavy periods: change pad or tampon "every 1 to 2 hours, or empty your menstrual cup more often than is recommended"; "use 2 types of period product together"; periods "lasting more than 7 days"; clots "larger than about 2.5cm (the size of a 10p coin)"; "bleed through to your clothes or bedding"; "avoid daily activities … or take time off work"; "feel tired or short of breath a lot". The NHS says to see a GP if periods affect your life, have been heavy for some time, come with severe pain, if you bleed between periods or after sex, or have other symptoms. — [NHS Heavy periods](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heavy-periods/)
- Endometriosis UK tells patients to download its Pain & Symptoms Diary to "remember relevant information", and says that "sharing as much information as possible during your first appointment will hopefully help speed up your diagnosis". — [Endometriosis UK: Visiting your doctor](https://www.endometriosis-uk.org/visiting-your-doctor)
- Endometriosis UK "Getting a diagnosis" (Jan 2025) advises keeping a pain and symptoms diary to show whether symptoms follow the cycle. It also advises describing the impact on daily life and pain location, intensity, whether pain is constant or cyclical, and its effect on work. (Summary of the PDF; the exact wording was only partly extracted.) — [Endometriosis UK Getting a diagnosis PDF](https://www.endometriosis-uk.org/sites/default/files/2025-01/Getting%20a%20diagnosis%20Jan%202025%20(3).pdf)
- Endometriosis UK search-result summary: also record how long symptoms have lasted and their intensity on a 0–10 scale. — [Endometriosis UK](https://www.endometriosis-uk.org/visiting-your-doctor) (from a search snippet, not confirmed on-page)
- PCOS (no NICE guideline; the international guideline is the reference). The 2023 International Evidence-based PCOS Guideline defines irregular cycles as follows: "1 to <3 years post menarche: <21 or >45 days"; "3 years post menarche to perimenopause: <21 or >35 days or <8 cycles per year"; "1 year post menarche >90 days for any 1 cycle"; "Primary amenorrhoea by age 15 or >3 years post thelarche". Clinicians should assess "acne, female pattern hair loss and hirsutism in adults". They should screen for depression in all and anxiety in adults, consider eating disorders "regardless of weight", seek permission to discuss psychosexual function, be aware of body image, and use weight-inclusive language. — [Teede et al., JCEM 2023](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/108/10/2447/7242360)
- Conflict: one search summary claimed acne is "not included" as a PCOS criterion in 2023. The JCEM guideline text above lists acne as a feature to assess. Treat acne as a history item. — [JCEM 2023](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/108/10/2447/7242360)
- US: ACOG published a new endometriosis Clinical Practice Guideline in Feb/Mar 2026, framed as shortening time to diagnosis. Per search snippets, it says to suspect endometriosis with at least one "cyclic or noncyclic" sign: chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dysuria, dyschezia, or infertility with any of these. — [ACOG news release 2026](https://www.acog.org/news/news-releases/2026/02/acog-publishes-new-endometriosis-clinical-guidance-aiming-shorten-time-diagnosis-improve-access-care); [Obstet Gynecol 2026 abstract](https://journals.lww.com/greenjournal/abstract/2026/03000/diagnosis_of_endometriosis__acog_clinical_practice.25.aspx) (full text not accessed)

### Inferences
- The best evidence for "what a GP wants" is the NICE history list itself. A summary that maps one-to-one onto NG73 1.3.1 and NG88 1.2.1 fields (pain with periods and its effect on daily life, pain during/after sex, bowel symptoms, urinary symptoms, bleeding pattern, bleeding between periods or after sex, pressure symptoms, impact, previous treatments, family history) saves the GP work without Atossa naming a condition. The fields can be labelled neutrally, e.g. "Pain when opening bowels: during period only", never "possible endometriosis".
- The NHS heavy-period criteria are all lay-reportable (pad-change interval, double protection, >7 days, clots bigger than a 10p coin, flooding onto clothes/bedding, missed activities, tiredness/breathlessness). They make good capture questions and summary fields.
- For PCOS, the useful things to capture are cycle length and variability (the numbers above), cycles per year, longest gap, years since first period, excess hair / acne / scalp hair thinning, and mood/eating/body-image impact if the user chooses to share. Atossa should report raw facts ("cycles ranged 28–62 days; 6 periods in last 12 months"). It must not flag them against the thresholds, since that would be diagnostic.
- "Family history of endometriosis (first-degree relative)" is now an explicit NICE 2024 item. It belongs in health history.
- NG73 1.3.4 supports quoting the patient's own words (verbatim descriptions) next to structured fields.

### Gaps
- NICE CKS pages (endometriosis, menorrhagia, PCOS) were blocked (403), so there are no CKS-level history checklists here.
- No NICE guideline exists for PCOS. The UK relies on CKS and the international guideline. RCOG Green-top guidance on PCOS was not checked.
- I found no study measuring how much of a 10-minute GP consultation is spent on history for gynaecological complaints.
- Verity (PCOS charity) and the Endometriosis Foundation of America resources were not reached.

## 2. Structured frameworks (SOCRATES, NRS, PBAC, EHP-5, menstrual diary) and which can be asked conversationally without being diagnostic

### Takeaway
SOCRATES and a 0–10 NRS map cleanly onto plain-language questions and are not diagnostic. EHP-5-style impact questions work if paraphrased. The validated EHP-5 is licensed, so its exact wording should not be copied. PBAC is poorly suited to primary care and modern period products. The NHS lay heavy-bleeding markers are a better conversational substitute.

### Cited Findings
- SOCRATES = Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associations, Time course/Timing, Exacerbating/relieving factors, Severity (often "on a scale of one to ten"). It is used to characterise pain and "adapts to almost any symptom". — [Skills Training Group](https://www.skillstg.co.uk/blog/socrates-pain-assessment-acronym/); [ClinicalBridge](https://clinicalbridge.ai/blog/socrates-medical-history-taking) (secondary/educational sources)
- The Endometriosis Symptom Diary (ESD), an ePRO, scores pain on a 0–10 numeric rating scale. Electronic diaries capture data in real time between visits. — [ESD item-importance study (PMC7789138)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7789138/); [Daily electronic Endometriosis Pain and Bleeding Diary (PMC2908571)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2908571/) (from search snippets)
- EHP-5 has a 5-item core covering pain, control and powerlessness, emotional well-being, social support and self-image. Responses are Never/Rarely/Sometimes/Often/Always, scored 0 (best) to 100 (worst). There are 6 more modular items (the full version has 11). — [Jones et al. 2004, EHP-5 development](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15130031/); [Iranian validation, J Ovarian Res](https://ovarianresearch.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1757-2215-4-11)
- PBAC: the original Higham 1990 chart reported a score ≥100 gave >80% sensitivity and specificity for >80 mL loss. — [Higham et al. 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2400752/)
- PBAC systematic review: across 9 validation studies, sensitivity was 58–97%, specificity 7.5–95.5%, and cut-offs ranged 50–185. "PBACs are best suited to the controlled and specific environment of clinical studies"; "Current lack of standardization precludes widespread use of the PBAC in primary care." Modern ultra-slim towels with superabsorbent polymers make surface stains hard to judge. Charts underestimate at high loss levels. — [Pictorial methods systematic review (PMC7011238)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7011238/)
- More than 10% of women with self-perceived normal bleeding had PBAC >100, anaemia or needed iron (so self-perception alone is also imperfect). — [ASH Blood abstract, PBAC reference range](https://ashpublications.org/blood/article/122/21/4772/12898/Establishment-Of-a-Reference-Range-For-The-Pbac) (from a search snippet)
- NICE NG88 1.1.1 favours quality-of-life impact over blood-loss measurement (see Section 1). — [NICE NG88](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Recommendations)

### Inferences
Suggested conversational mapping (all descriptive, none name a cause):
- **Site**: "Where do you feel it?" Offer a body-map tap or choices: lower tummy, one side, lower back, deep inside, legs, bottom/rectum.
- **Onset / duration**: "When did this start? Months or years?" and "Has it changed over time?" Duration drives referral thinking, and "years" is key context.
- **Character**: offer patient words (cramping, stabbing, burning, dragging, aching). Keep the user's verbatim phrase.
- **Radiation**: "Does it spread anywhere?"
- **Associations**: pain when opening bowels, pain or blood when passing urine, pain during/after sex, bloating, nausea, fatigue. These are NG73 items phrased as experiences.
- **Timing vs cycle**: "Is it only around your period, at other times too, or all the time?" This is the most important field for GPs, and Endometriosis UK stresses cyclical pattern.
- **Exacerbating/relieving**: "What helps? What have you tried (painkillers, the pill…) and did it help?" This records previous treatments (NG88 1.2.1) without recommending any.
- **Severity**: 0–10 NRS, "worst" and "typical" separately.
- **Impact**: days missed from work/school/social plans, time in bed, sleep. Plus EHP-5-inspired (paraphrased, not copied) prompts on feeling in control, mood, relationships and self-image. Check licensing before using official EHP-5 wording or scoring (Oxford University Innovation holds EHP rights; I did not verify current licence terms).
- **Bleeding**: skip PBAC scoring. Ask the NHS markers instead: how often products are changed on the heaviest day, double protection, days bleeding, clots bigger than a 10p coin, leaking onto clothes/bedding at night, bleeding between periods or after sex, tiredness/breathlessness.
- **PCOS-type**: cycle dates (gives lengths automatically), hair growth on face/chest/stomach, acne, scalp hair thinning, and optionally mood and eating. Report these as observations only.
- None of these questions is diagnostic in itself. The diagnostic risk is in the output (thresholds, scores labelled "suggestive of", condition names). Show raw values ("worst pain 8/10") and no interpretive bands.

### Gaps
- No authoritative (NICE/RCOG) source for SOCRATES was found. It is a standard UK medical-school mnemonic; the citations here are educational websites.
- OPQRST (US/EMS variant: Onset, Provocation, Quality, Region/Radiation, Severity, Time) was not separately sourced.
- EHP-5 licensing terms were not verified.
- I found no validated conversational or chatbot adaptation of SOCRATES for patients.

## 3. Existing tools and templates, and clinician reception of patient app exports

### Takeaway
Existing tools are mostly paper diaries (Endometriosis UK, Bupa) or raw exports (Bearable exports CSV and has no polished doctor PDF). Research on clinicians is consistent: they value patient data but have no time, distrust raw volume, and want concise summaries. Some flatly ignore long printouts.

### Cited Findings
- Endometriosis UK Pain & Symptoms Diary (2022) and "Getting Diagnosed" guide (2024/2025) are downloadable PDFs. — [Endometriosis UK](https://www.endometriosis-uk.org/visiting-your-doctor)
- Bupa publishes a separate "Endometriosis symptoms diary". — [Bupa diary PDF](https://www.bupa.co.uk/~/media/Files/MMS/bins-05609) (not opened)
- The Menstrual Health Project published an "Endometriosis Diagnostic Toolkit" (May 2025). — [Menstrual Health Project toolkit PDF](https://menstrualhealthproject.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Endometriosis-Diagnosis-Toolkit.pdf) (not opened; worth reviewing)
- Bearable: "Export My Data" gives CSV for 3 months, 6 months or all time. Users have requested "a neat health report" PDF, and screenshots are the current workaround. — [Bearable support](https://bearable.app/support/common-questions/how-to-share-data-with-a-doctor-therapist-or-other-medical-team-member/); [Bearable roadmap: PDF export](https://changemap.co/bearable-/bearable-roadmap/task/4070-pdf-export/)
- Finnish endometriosis app study (qualitative: 16 patients, 13 HCPs, 4 patient-org reps, 43 written stories, 2021–23). One patient said: "I had printed several months of statistics, how bad the situation was, but the doctor wouldn't even look at it". One clinician said: "Sometimes I think it becomes never-ending, they monitor the body too much, every instance of pain". Specialist endometriosis nurses valued tracking for assessment and for evaluating treatment. "Many clinicians do not consider patient-provided digital information as clinically meaningful." — [Oikkonen & Temmes 2025 (PMC12675829)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12675829/)
- Korean physician survey (n=157 plus 6 interviews): 53% had seen symptom diaries. 65% of PGHD arrived as handwritten paper, 31% digitally. Barriers: lack of EHR integration or of a summary (31.2%), data accuracy concerns (ranked top), time/workflow (24.2%), lack of guidelines (22.9%). Physicians wanted summary or visual dashboards rather than raw data. — [JMIR 2025/26 physician PGHD survey (PMC13537431)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13537431/)
- Systematic review (Lordon et al. 2020, 13 publications): PGHD supported communication and awareness. Patients wanted clinicians to engage with their data, and clinicians found this hard to accommodate. — [Lordon et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1460458220928184)
- Clinicians were concerned about workload and about errors from data overload. They said good design that summarises data to fit routine workflow mitigates this, and noted the absence of summary dashboards as a barrier. — [Frontiers: Information quality challenges of PGD (PMC5701635)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5701635/); [PGHD review (PMC10971637)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10971637/) (via search summary)
- Systematic review of attitudes (33 studies): most HCPs are positive about PGHD, but security, reliability and workflow integration concerns persist. — [PMC11955519](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11955519/) (search snippet)
- Around 3 in 4 menstrual-app users would share symptom data with clinicians. Clinicians cite lack of integration into digital health infrastructure. — [Curr Opin Endocrinol review, symptom tracking in menstrual apps](https://doi.org/10.1097/MED.0000000000000682) (search snippet)

### Inferences
- The main failure mode is "several months of statistics" that doesn't get read. Atossa's differentiator should be a short, pre-digested page, not more data. Keep raw logs as an optional appendix.
- Accuracy/trust concerns suggest the PDF should state its provenance: "Patient-reported via Atossa; not clinically verified; date range X–Y; N entries". It should separate what the patient reported on the day from later recollection.
- Guava Health and other "doctor report" layouts were not examined, so there is no evidence on them here.

### Gaps
- Guava, Clue, Flo, Phendo and elleFA doctor-report layouts were not reviewed. elleFA has a feasibility trial on improving patient–physician dialogue ([NCT06452407](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06452407)), and results were not found.
- No UK GP-specific study of patient app exports for gynaecological symptoms was found.

## 4. Layout best practice for clinicians: order, timeline visuals, length, what to leave out

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin and mostly from other specialties. It supports a concise, summary-first, visually simple report. Bar charts and heat maps work better than line graphs for individual-level data, and raw data belongs out of the main view.

### Cited Findings
- Systematic review of PRO visualisation: for individual-level data, bar charts were preferred over line graphs in 3 studies and heat maps in 1. Clinician comprehension of individual-level bar and line charts was 90–100%. — [Comprehension of and preferences for PRO visualisation, systematic review (Patient Educ Couns 2026)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0738399126001618) (search snippet)
- Pediatric asthma participatory design: providers wanted more detail on demand, additional sources, and visual integration with EHR data. Visual synthesis of multiple PGHD elements helped interpretation. — [Provider preferences for PGHD displays, PubMed 34289505](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34289505/)
- Physicians prefer summary/visualised dashboards to raw data (see Section 3). — [PMC13537431](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13537431/)
- Nurse sonographer in a UK HCP focus-group study: GP referrals can say "'chronic pelvic pain, ?cyst'" even when the patient described cyclical pain. The cyclical pattern gets lost in transmission. — [HCP perspectives on endometriosis delay (PMC12629012)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12629012/)

### Inferences (design recommendations, not directly evidenced)
- **Length**: one page for the at-a-glance summary, readable in about 60 seconds. Appendices (daily log, full entries) on later pages or collapsed in-app.
- **Order** (mirrors the GP history structure and the NICE lists):
  1. Header: name, DOB/age, date range covered, number of entries, "patient-reported" label.
  2. "What I'd like from today": the patient's own 1–2 lines (main concern and question). This fits the patient-agenda approach, but I found no specific study.
  3. Main symptoms at a glance: each with duration (months/years), relation to cycle (period-only / cyclical / constant), typical and worst 0–10, and frequency.
  4. Cycle and bleeding: cycle length range, period length, cycles in last 12 months, heaviest-day product changes, clots >10p, flooding, bleeding between periods or after sex.
  5. Impact: days missed from work/school in the period, sleep, activities stopped, and a brief mood line if shared.
  6. What's been tried: medicines/contraception, doses if known, and whether they helped (patient-reported). No recommendations.
  7. Relevant history: family history (e.g. a first-degree relative with a diagnosed condition, reported as the patient's own words), previous investigations and appointments.
  8. Patient's own words: 1–3 short verbatim quotes.
- **Timeline visual**: one cycle-aligned strip or heat-map (days × symptom, with period days shaded) so the cyclical pattern is visible at a glance. This addresses the "?cyst" problem where the pattern gets lost. Prefer bars and heat maps over line charts.
- **Leave out**: interpretive labels, scores with severity bands, condition names, "likely/possibly", unlabelled raw tallies, long free text, wellness fluff (mood emojis, streaks). Also exclude optional sensitive data (sexual/mental health) unless the user opts in per export.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT or usability study specific to gynaecology one-page summaries for GPs.
- Evidence that a patient-agenda line or verbatim quotes improve consultations was not searched.

## 5. Diagnostic delay context, and how documentation helps

### Takeaway
UK endometriosis diagnosis now takes about 8 years 10 months on average (Endometriosis UK 2024). Contributors include normalisation of symptoms, repeated GP visits, short appointments and pain being hard to "see". Clear documentation of cyclicity and impact directly addresses these, though there is no trial evidence that diaries shorten delay.

### Cited Findings
- Endometriosis UK survey (n=4,371, March 2024): average diagnosis time 8 years 10 months in England and Scotland (up 10 months since 2020), 9y 5m in Northern Ireland, 9y 11m in Wales. 78% were told they were making a "fuss about nothing" or similar. 47% visited a GP 10+ times. Only 10% of GPs mentioned suspected endometriosis at the first or second appointment. 20% saw a gynaecologist 10+ times. — [Endometriosis UK diagnosis report](https://www.endometriosis-uk.org/diagnosis-report); [Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/average-endometriosis-diagnosis-delay-now-almost-9-years-2024a10004ax)
- A University of York study (2024) reports an average of almost 7 years. The figure depends on method and population. — [University of York news](https://www.york.ac.uk/news-and-events/news/2024/research/diagnosis-endometriosis-delay/)
- UK HCP focus groups (15 professionals: 5 GPs, 5 gynaecologists, 5 nurses). A gynaecologist said: "it's quite difficult to ascertain all the concerns in a 10-minute appointment". A GP said: "it is easy to normalise it as a doctor". Another GP: "You can't visualise pain and so it makes it difficult to appreciate the severity… doesn't make you want to refer". The presence of a partner "adds weight and gravity to the psychosocial impact" and prompted reconsideration of referral. — [PMC12629012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12629012/)
- NG73 1.1.2 states that delays "can affect quality of life". — [NICE NG73](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)

### Inferences
- The GP quotes point to exactly what a summary can supply: making pain "visible" (worst/typical 0–10 over time, days missed) and showing impact in the way a partner's testimony does (concrete, third-person-readable consequences such as "missed 3 workdays in each of the last 4 cycles").
- With repeat visits common (47% at 10+ GP visits), a "since last appointment" view and a log of previous appointments and outcomes ("what's been tried") are high value. They stop the patient having to restart the story each time.
- Frame the product as helping the patient describe symptoms clearly, not as speeding up diagnosis. Claims about faster diagnosis are unevidenced and edge towards diagnostic framing.

### Gaps
- I found no study showing that symptom diaries or app summaries shorten time to diagnosis.
- No PCOS diagnostic-delay figure was gathered.
