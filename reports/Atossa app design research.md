# Rebuild Atossa as a quiet clinical notebook

The leading apps in this space are built around prediction or engagement. Atossa's opening is to be built around the record. Cycle trackers put a ring and a "days until" number on the home screen, use a wall of chips for logging, and lock the doctor report behind a paywall. Chronic-illness trackers win on few taps and lose users when a check-in takes ten minutes. AI journals and symptom checkers show that people like conversational capture but give thinner accounts to a bot unless it asks good follow-up questions. GPs, meanwhile, ignore "several months of statistics" and want one page that answers the history questions NICE already tells them to ask. So Atossa should work like a **quiet clinical notebook**. The home screen has one big voice button. Capture is a short conversation that ends in an editable **"Here's what I noted"** card, which quotes the user's own words and never interprets them. There is a timeline of notes, and a **one-page, print-ready GP summary** organised around NICE's endometriosis and heavy-bleeding history items. Visually, I recommend one direction, **"Warm Paper, Deep Ink"**. It uses a warm off-white canvas, near-black warm ink and a single deep plum accent, and every other colour has a fixed meaning. Inter carries the UI, and the already-installed Instrument Serif is kept only for the user's quoted words. The app stays light-only, which honours the 2026-09-24 decisions to drop Fraunces and dark mode rather than reversing them. This answers each of the six problems in the current audit: no home screen, colours that mean nothing, serif/bold clashes, flat lists, hidden voice, and a doctor output that does not look clinical. It also keeps every screen clear of diagnosis, probability, cause, treatment and prediction.

## Cycle trackers sell prediction, which Atossa must not copy

Mainstream period apps share one design: a **cycle-status hero** (a ring, circle or blob with the cycle day or "Period in 2 days"), a "+" log button, and content cards below. Flo's dashboard is "action-oriented, immediately showing the user their current cycle status" ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/flo-period-pregnancy-tracker)). Clue's hero is a segmented ring reading "2 days until your next period", and its five-tab bar has a filled "+" Track button in the centre ([Clue App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/clue-period-cycle-tracker/id657189652)). Natural Cycles' hero is a single green "Not fertile" or red "Use Protection" circle ([NC Help](https://help.naturalcycles.com/hc/en-us/articles/360003339574-What-are-Red-and-Green-Days)). These heroes exist because the products sell forecasts. Hormona itself admits its hormone-curve home screen "differs for those with irregular cycles" ([Hormona](https://www.hormona.io/products/hormona-cycle-app)). A competitor blog claims calendar prediction "struggle[s] with PCOS" ([Go Go Gaia](https://www.go-go-gaia.com/blog/best-pcos-tracking-app.html)); that source is not neutral, but it matches Clue reviewers' complaints that predictions are poor until months of data build up ([Clue App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/clue-period-ovulation-tracker/id657189652)). For a PCOS or endometriosis audience, a ring is both off-mission and alienating. If Atossa copied it, the design would carry prediction in even with every number removed.

Logging across the category follows **"+" → a grid of icon chips by category → Apply**. Flo has 16 trackers and 70+ options ([Flo Help](https://help.flo.health/hc/en-us/articles/4406826542740-Logging-your-symptoms)). Clue has 200+ options in categories users can reorder ([Clue Support](https://support.helloclue.com/hc/en-us/articles/214434586-How-do-I-add-delete-or-rearrange-my-tracking-categories)). Clue's 2022 relaunch added "Spotting" on the principle that "not all bleeding is period bleeding" ([Clue relaunch](https://helloclue.com/articles/how-to-use-clue/clue-period-tracking-relaunched-and-newly-designed)), which matters for this audience. Apple Health's shape code is the most honest calendar grammar available: solid red for logged periods, striped for predicted, dots for other data ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120356)). Atossa can use the "logged" and "dot" states and simply leave out the "predicted" state, and that absence makes its honesty visible.

The praise and complaints are consistent. Flo users object that "every so many minutes…it prompts me to purchase" and that features moved behind a paywall ([Flo App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/flo-cycle-period-tracker/id1038369065)). Clue users mourned a redesign that removed customisation ([Clue App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/clue-period-ovulation-tracker/id657189652)). Stardust is praised for its looks but judged "high in emotional engagement but falls short in clinical depth" ([SheRanked](https://www.sheranked.com/app-reviews/stardust-)). The doctor report is **paywalled at Flo and Clue** ([Flo Help](https://help.flo.health/hc/en-us/articles/360042141812-What-is-included-in-Flo-Premium)). Flo Premium also includes the pattern Atossa must never build: a symptom checker that shows "how closely you match common symptoms of conditions like PCOS and endometriosis". Its App Store frame lists "PCOS – Significant match" ([Flo App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/flo-cycle-period-tracker/id1038369065)). Every existing report is cycle-centric. None turns a person's own description into a structured narrative, so that gap is open. Flo's ~55-step onboarding with a "tap and hold" commitment gesture before the paywall ([ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/flo-period-pregnancy-tracker)) is a pattern to avoid: a short, free onboarding is a real difference in this market.

## Chronic-illness apps win on few taps and lose at ten minutes

The symptom trackers people keep using make a check-in a few taps long. Bearable pitches logging "with just a few taps each day" for users with brain fog, and lets them hide categories to start small ([Bearable](https://bearable.app/)). Its failure mode is also documented: "super complex and takes a lot of time to setup", with some users spending **more than 10 minutes per entry** because there are "too many options" ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bearable-symptom-tracker/id1482581097?see-all=reviews); [ChoosingTherapy](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/bearable-app-review/)). A reviewer who tested endo apps for three months found Flutter "most detailed" but skipped days because it was so broad ([Endometriosis.net](https://endometriosis.net/living/apps)). Visible's redesigned Today page makes the opposite bet: one hero arc and nothing else. One user explains why: "On bad brain fog days, I don't have the energy to interpret lots of information. I can glance once and immediately understand where I am" ([Visible blog](https://www.makevisible.com/blog/introducing-updated-today-page)). Accessibility guidance agrees: "dense dashboards and multi-step check-ins require executive capacity—the very resource that… brain fog often reduce[s]" ([Pharma Marketing Network](https://www.pharma-mkting.com/articles/neuroinclusive-patient-app-ux-designing-every-mind/)). The same guidance recommends voice as a primary input and a visible button for every gesture ([Smashing 2026](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2026/07/designing-distressed-users-mental-health-apps-ui/)).

The better apps structure information by separating **logging, looking back and records**. Bearable has Home, Insights and More ([ChoosingTherapy](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/bearable-app-review/)). Guava keeps medications, conditions and allergies in a Records tab, away from daily logging ([Guava](https://guavahealth.com/news/visit-prep-summary-announcement)). Atossa's current Dashboard crams stats, two charts, a symptom list, a calendar, appointments, medicines and history into one scroll, which is the anti-pattern these apps avoid. In endometriosis apps, pain capture is settling on a **0–10 rating plus named body regions**: pelvis, lower back/sacrum, legs, shoulder, and pain with sex ([Endometriosis Tracker](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/endometriosis-tracker/id6743563939); [Endolog](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/endolog-endometriosis-tracker/id6756697164)). Phendo, built with endo patients through participatory design, adds activities of daily living that were hard to do and a journal ([Phendo](https://arxiv.org/html/1811.03431v1)).

Trend displays are where these apps drift toward diagnosis. Bearable markets "how your habits… improve & worsen your health" ([Bearable](https://bearable.app/)). One of its users notes the correlation grid has no numbers, "potentially leading to misleading conclusions" ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bearable-symptom-tracker/id1482581097?see-all=reviews)). Endometriosis Diary advertises "cause and effect relationship" detection ([Endometriosis.net](https://endometriosis.net/living/apps)). Atossa's safe options are counts, dates, severity-over-time and co-occurrence shown visually, with the reading left to the GP. Guidance on streaks points the same way: streaks should not reset after one missed day, missed days should be treated as normal, and there should be no visible "failure" record ([Smashing 2026](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2026/07/designing-distressed-users-mental-health-apps-ui/)). A 2025 JMIR review found endo apps scored well on visual design (73.7%) but poorly on "therapeutic alliance", the supportive language people need (47.4%) ([JMIR 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11845897/)). Tone is therefore where most apps in the category fall short.

## Voice capture works when the machine mirrors instead of interpreting

The evidence favours a **hybrid**: conversational pacing with tappable answers and a fast way out. In a within-subject study (N=206), **69.9% preferred a health-intake chatbot** over a form, but the chatbot was slower (median **212.5 s vs 123.0 s**). Users called the form "old-school" ([Soni et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9606606/)). Reviews of symptom-checker chatbots name the main frustrations: rigid options ("there has to be an option for 'at times'"), questions in an illogical order, and **no explanation of why a question is asked**. Users valued tracking symptoms over time ([You & Gui](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8075525/)). NN/g's 2026 chatbot guidelines add: make suggestions clickable buttons, don't autoscroll, support save and share, and offer voice. One tester said "please, please have a voice-to-text option…I would have actually shut this page off" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-chatbots-design-guidelines/)). How We Feel makes every step after the first optional, and a long press saves and skips the rest ([How We Feel](https://howwefeel.substack.com/p/ch-ch-ch-changes)). Daylio's two-tap entry is praised because people can log "even when they are at their worst" ([IntuitionLabs](https://intuitionlabs.ai/software/telepsychiatry-digital-mental-health/mood-tracking-and-journaling/daylio)).

There are two findings about completeness. Google's SymptomAI study (n=13,917) found that agents which **actively asked structured history questions** did significantly better than letting users describe freely ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/symptomai-towards-a-conversational-ai-agent-for-everyday-symptom-assessment/)). SymptomAI ends in a differential diagnosis, which Atossa must not do; only its question-asking finding carries over. A second study reports that symptom reports were about 8% less suitable for urgency assessment when people believed they were talking to an AI ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404343790_Reduced_symptom_reporting_quality_during_human-chatbot_versus_human-physician_interactions)). That figure comes from a search snippet, so it is directional only. Together they suggest Atossa should explicitly invite a messy, narrative first answer, and then ask only the follow-ups the answer left unfilled.

Voice UI has settled. In November 2025 ChatGPT moved voice out of a full-screen orb, which showed no text, and into the chat thread with a live transcript ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/11/26/chatgpt-voice-mode-update-seamless-chat/)). Rosebud's voice sheet is almost empty: "Listening…", a waveform, and round ✕ and ✓ buttons ([Rosebud App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rosebud-ai-journal-diary/id6451135127)). Developers agree the transcript must be editable, because no speech model is perfect ([VP0](https://vp0.com/blogs/whisper-voice-transcription-app-ui-swiftui)). For confirmation, Granola is the model to copy. It shows the user's own notes in black and AI additions **in grey**, "so you always know what you wrote versus what the AI added", and each line links back to the transcript it came from ([Granola docs](https://docs.granola.ai/help-center/taking-notes/ai-enhanced-notes)). Rosebud shows the opposite risk. Critics say chat-first journaling produces entries that are "transcripts of the AI's questions rather than your own thinking" ([Mindsera](https://mindsera.com/articles/the-7-best-ai-journaling-apps-in-2026-tested)). For Atossa the user's words are the product, so the confirmation card must lead with them.

Rewards should come from accumulation, not loss aversion. Duolingo's streaks are called "weaponized" guilt ([Medium](https://medium.com/@Jason-Han/the-architecture-of-guilt-how-duolingo-weaponized-negative-emotion-to-build-a-design-system-a1ea491e7813)). Finch is praised because it "never pressures you or punishes you for missing a day" ([HabitBox](https://habitbox.app/blog/finch-app-review)). Rosebud lets users change an entry's date after the fact ([Rosebud help](https://help.rosebud.app/daily-journaling/streaks)). For a symptom audience, bad days are exactly the days that matter. An app that makes a missed day feel like failure is working against its own data.

## A GP reads one page that follows the NICE history

The most direct evidence of what a GP needs is the history NICE already asks them to take. NG73 says to suspect endometriosis with any of the following: chronic pelvic pain; period pain "affecting daily activities and quality of life"; deep pain during or after sex; period-related or cyclical bowel symptoms (especially painful bowel movements); cyclical urinary symptoms; or infertility alongside any of these. Since 2024 it also asks about first-degree family history. It tells clinicians that "keeping a pain and symptom diary can aid discussions" ([NICE NG73](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)). NG88 on heavy bleeding asks for "the nature of the bleeding; related symptoms… impact on her quality of life; … previous treatment", and puts quality of life ahead of measured blood loss ([NICE NG88](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Recommendations)). The NHS markers for heavy periods can all be reported by the patient. They include changing products every 1–2 hours, using two products at once, bleeding for more than 7 days, clots larger than a 10p coin, and bleeding through onto clothes or bedding ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heavy-periods/)). These are better conversational questions than the PBAC chart, which a systematic review says is unsuitable for primary care ([PMC7011238](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7011238/)). For PCOS, the 2023 international guideline makes cycle length and variability, acne, hair loss and hirsutism assessable features, and asks for weight-inclusive language ([Teede et al.](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/108/10/2447/7242360)). Atossa should report raw facts such as "cycles ranged 28–62 days; 6 periods in 12 months". It must never measure them against the guideline thresholds, because that would be diagnosis.

Clinician research explains why most exports fail. In a Finnish study, one patient said "I had printed several months of statistics… but the doctor wouldn't even look at it", and a clinician worried that patients "monitor the body too much, every instance of pain" ([Oikkonen & Temmes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12675829/)). In a physician survey (n=157), **data accuracy was the top concern**. Doctors asked for summaries or visual dashboards rather than raw data ([PMC13537431](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13537431/)). A systematic review found that clinicians could read individual-level bar and line charts 90–100% of the time, and preferred **bar charts and heat maps over line graphs** ([Patient Educ Couns 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0738399126001618)). UK health professionals describe the specific loss Atossa can fix. A patient's cyclical pain becomes "chronic pelvic pain, ?cyst" in a referral. A GP says "you can't visualise pain… doesn't make you want to refer" ([PMC12629012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12629012/)). The stakes are large: UK diagnosis takes an average of **8 years 10 months**, 47% of patients see a GP 10+ times, and 78% were told they were making a "fuss about nothing" ([Endometriosis UK](https://www.endometriosis-uk.org/diagnosis-report)).

From this the summary's shape follows. Guava's visit prep sets the benchmark for a one-page summary: choose a visit type, and it pulls in symptoms with **onset date and average severity**, the patient's questions, and meds and allergies ([Guava](https://guavahealth.com/news/visit-prep-summary-announcement)). Atossa should build page one in this order. First, a header with the date range, the entry count and the words "patient-reported, not a diagnosis". Second, "What I'd like from today" in the patient's own one or two lines. Third, main symptoms, each with how long it has been going on, its relation to the period (period-only, cyclical, or constant), typical and worst 0–10, and days logged. Fourth, bleeding, using the NHS markers. Fifth, impact on daily life (days missed, time in bed, sleep). Sixth, what has been tried and whether it helped. Seventh, relevant history, including family history in the patient's words. Last, a cycle-aligned heat-strip that makes the cyclical pattern impossible to miss. Page two is optional and holds one to three dated verbatim quotes and the log. NG73 1.3.4 recognises that pain "may be expressed in different ways", which supports quoting patients verbatim ([NICE NG73](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations)). The summary should present itself as helping the patient describe symptoms clearly. It should not claim to speed up diagnosis, because no study shows diaries shorten diagnostic delay, and that claim edges toward diagnostic framing.

## Premium health design is one ground, one accent and colour with meaning

In the App Store survey, the apps that look premium show restraint: one dominant ground, one accent, light-weight or serif display type, one hero object per screen, cards without borders, and muted data colours. The cheap-looking ones use collages, rainbow category colours, dense walls of chips, and alarm-red gauges ([Oura](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/oura/id1043837948); [How We Feel](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/how-we-feel/id1562706384); [Welltory](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/welltory-stress-heart-rate/id1074367771)). The "Endometriosis Tracker" is the clearest warning. It is CareClinic's template recoloured magenta, with a heavy black serif on a gradient, and was rated about one star ([Endo Tracker](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/endometriosis-tracker/id6743563939)). Oura's redesign cut five tabs to three (Today, Vitals, My Health) ([Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/new-app-design/)). It uses a "semantic color system" and three data tiers: glance, then focused metrics, then exploration ([Instrument](https://www.instrument.com/work/oura-app)). Oura's reverse-engineered web tokens pair a warm cream (`#f7f1e8`) with Akkurat, a grotesk, and flip **a single phrase per heading** to Editorial New italic ([oh-my-design](https://oh-my-design.kr/design-systems/oura); [shadcn.io](https://www.shadcn.io/design/oura)). Whoop's palette is "intentionally narrow", and "every hue carries meaning" ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/whoop-design-breakdown)).

Femtech is openly moving away from pink. Midi Health's August 2026 rebrand dropped "the softer aesthetic often associated with women's health" in favour of bold type and high contrast ([Femtech Insider](https://femtechinsider.com/midi-health-expands-beyond-menopause-care-with-new-brand-identity/)). Headspace rebuilt its palette partly for accessible contrast ([It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/italic-studio-headspace-graphic-design-project-250424)). Guidance for distressed users sets 4.5:1 contrast for body text, recommends muted earthy tones, and warns against both saturated palettes and low-contrast minimalism ([Smashing 2026](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2026/07/designing-distressed-users-mental-health-apps-ui/)). On iOS 26, Liquid Glass "belongs to your controls and navigation… not to the content itself", and health content backgrounds should stay opaque ([Mindbowser](https://www.mindbowser.com/ios-26-liquid-glass-react-native-health-app-guide/)). iOS conventions set a 34 pt large title that collapses to 17 pt, 17 pt body text and 44×44 pt touch targets ([LearnUI](https://www.learnui.design/blog/ios-design-guidelines-templates.html)).

Measured against this, Atossa's current system fails for identifiable reasons. The cream canvas is fine; Oura and Headspace use almost the same colour. The problems are elsewhere. Salmon `#f6b0a6`, mint and sky are three decorative accents with no meaning, and the selected tab changes colour from tab to tab. Salmon on the cream canvas measures **1.72:1**, so it cannot carry text or controls. Work Sans 700 is used everywhere, so nothing stands out, and Instrument Serif is used as a heading style rather than as rare emphasis. The Chat tab opens to about 60% empty space with the mic as a small grey square. The Dashboard is a data dump with four stat tiles of equal weight. The doctor tab is a stack of accordions that does not look like a clinical document.

## Recommended direction: "Warm Paper, Deep Ink"

### Why this direction, and not the other two

I chose a light, warm, editorial-clinical system: a notebook a GP would take seriously. Three reasons decide it. First, the premium health references and the femtech trend both point to a warm neutral ground plus one grown-up accent with fixed meanings. Second, the doctor summary must print and read well in black and white, and a light UI lets the app and the PDF share one visual language. Third, it respects the 2026-09-24 decisions. It does not bring back Fraunces or dark mode. It keeps Instrument Serif, which is already installed, but limits it to one job. **"Calm Clinical Light"** (mist blue, navy, Ada-style serif prompts) was the runner-up. It signals trust quickly but drifts toward generic hospital-portal blue and loses warmth, and the category is already weak on warmth. **"Quiet Dusk"** (Oura or How We Feel dark plum-navy) is the most striking. I rejected it because it reverses a deliberate decision to remove dark mode, doubles the theming work, and needs a separate light document style for the GP. Keep its lesson, one serif line per hero, and structure the tokens so a warm-dark theme could be added later.

### Information architecture: three tabs and no chat tab

| Tab | Screen title | Job | Replaces |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Today** | "Today" (large title), avatar top-right opens Profile | Start a capture; see what is ready for the next appointment | Chat (as landing) and the Dashboard stat tiles |
| **Timeline** | "Timeline" | Every note, period, appointment and medicine as one filterable feed plus a month view | Dashboard's long scroll, the Period Log page, and the separate appointment/medicine/history sections |
| **For my doctor** | "For my doctor" | Build, preview and share the one-page GP summary | the current For my doctor accordions |

Capture is a **full-screen modal, not a tab**. It opens from the Today hero, from a "+" in Timeline, and from any "Add more" link, so the conversation never sits as an empty tab. Medicines, appointments and health history become **entry types** in Timeline, and are edited from a "Records" section in Profile, following Guava's split. Tab bar: three items with a 1.75 pt line icon and an 11 pt label. The selected item is tinted plum; there is no coloured pill. The surface is near-opaque warm white (`rgba(255,255,255,0.94)`) with a hairline top border, or iOS 26's floating capsule if `expo-glass-effect` proves stable in SDK 57. Glass stays on navigation only.

### Core flow 1: Today, the home screen

The top of the screen is a greeting line in Inter ("Evening, Sam"). Below it is a single **hero card** containing a serif-italic prompt, "*What's been going on with your body?*", a **72 pt plum mic button** labelled "Tap to talk", and a quieter text link, "Type instead". Under the hero sits a row of four **starter chips**: "Pain today", "My period", "Something new", "Just a quick note". These follow NN/g's clickable suggestions and remove the empty-chat problem. Next comes a **"Rough day?" quick log**: one 0–10 row of large buttons and a save, which gives a 10-second entry on a bad day. Below that is the **appointment card**. With an appointment set, it reads "GP on Thu 9 Oct · 12 notes ready" with "Preview summary". Without one, it reads "Got an appointment coming up? Add it and we'll get your summary ready." Last is **"Your latest note"**, the most recent entry showing the user's own words in serif. There are no stat tiles, rings, streaks or charts on Today. The screen's one number is the note count toward the appointment.

### Core flow 2: capture by voice or text

The flow has five steps, with a quick save available at every one.

**1. Speak or type.** Tapping the mic opens the capture sheet with the prompt at the top and the helper line "Say it however it comes. Messy is fine." Recording is **tap to start, tap to stop**, because holding a button is hard when someone is in pain or needs time to describe something. A plum waveform shows level and an elapsed timer runs beside a round Stop button. The live transcript streams into the thread as the user's own bubble and does not autoscroll. The user can edit the transcript before continuing. Where speech recognition is unavailable (for example in Expo Go), the sheet falls back to the keyboard with the line "Voice isn't available here, so type instead. Your words are saved the same way."

**2. Follow-ups only for gaps.** Atossa asks only about SOCRATES/NICE fields the answer left empty: where it is, how strong (typical and worst), timing relative to the period, how long it has been going on, what it got in the way of, and what has been tried. Each question appears as a plain-text assistant line with **pill chips**, and always includes "Not sure", "It varies" and "Skip", plus a free-text/mic field. Each question also carries a one-line **"Why I'm asking"** in tertiary ink, such as "GPs usually ask this, so it helps to have it ready." A progress label reads "2 of up to 4" and never a percentage. Where pain is described, a **body-zone picker** with six named, tappable regions (lower tummy left/centre/right, lower back, deep inside, legs, bottom/when opening bowels) replaces freehand drawing. Anatomy illustration stays abstract, a simple outline with no organs.

**3. "Here's what I noted."** A confirmation card borrows Granola's two-tone rule. Each row has a small grey label (Where, What it felt like, How strong, When, Got in the way of, Tried) and the user's **exact phrase in ink**, quoted: "'like a hot knife, low down on the left'". Atossa's tidy value appears in ink-secondary beneath it ("Lower tummy, left · 8/10 at worst"). Tapping any row edits it. Empty fields appear as soft "+ Add when" chips, not errors. There is a date control, "Today ▾", so the user can say the note is about yesterday. The card contains no interpretation fields of any kind.

**4. Save.** The primary button reads "Looks right, save", with secondary options "Fix something" and "Add more". On save, the card shrinks and settles toward the For my doctor tab icon over 280 ms, a light success haptic fires, and a toast reads "Saved. 13 notes ready for your GP."

**5. Quick save.** "That's all for now" stays visible from step 2 onwards. It saves whatever exists, with the raw transcript always kept.

Two dependencies need noting. Filling fields from free speech needs an extraction model: the deployed `/chat` LLM, or a narrower extraction endpoint. Until that exists, the same card can be filled by the guided follow-ups alone. And because the no-diagnosis rule is currently enforced only in the prompt, the server needs a response filter before any model text is shown in this card.

### Core flow 3: Timeline

At the top is a segmented control: **List · Month**. **List** is a reverse-chronological feed grouped by week ("This week", "Last week", "Sept 8–14"). Each entry is a card with a type glyph, the time, the user's first line of words in serif, and plain chips for extracted facts ("Lower back · 6/10"). Period days show a clay left edge. Filter chips across the top read All · Symptoms · Bleeding · Appointments · Medicines. **Month** is a calendar with **no streak chain**. A small plum dot marks a day with a note, a solid clay fill marks a logged bleeding day, and a hatched clay outline marks logged spotting, echoing Apple's logged-state grammar. There is no predicted state. Missing days are blank, never red. A "Log a period" button sits at the top of Month. Tapping a day opens that day's entries in a sheet.

### Core flow 4: doctor summary and share

The screen has four steps. **Step 1, "What's this appointment for?"** offers chips for First time talking about this, Follow-up, Seeing a specialist and Other, borrowing Guava's visit types. **Step 2, the range**, is a segmented control: Since last appointment · 3 months · 6 months. **Step 3, "What I'd like from today"** is a two-line field that can be dictated, with a hint: "What's the one thing you want your GP to take away?" **Step 4, the preview**, is the **actual A4 page rendered as a white document card** at reduced scale, the "fanned white document" visual shorthand used by Visible and Guava. Tapping a section lets the user include or exclude it. Sensitive sections (sex, mood, eating) are **off by default** and must be switched on for each export. The primary action is **"Share PDF"** (share sheet), with "Print" and **"Show on screen"** as secondary actions. Show on screen is a large-type, high-contrast view the patient can hand across the desk. The PDF uses Inter and Instrument Serif in ink on white, with plum only in the heat-strip. It carries a footer on every page: "Patient-reported via Atossa · not clinically verified · 14 Jul–29 Sep 2026 · 23 entries."

The rules: plum means "you can act here" and nothing else. Clay means "blood was logged" and nothing else. Green and red are reserved for **system** status and never describe a body. Severity is a single-hue ramp, never traffic-light, and always shown with a digit. There are no gradients behind content. The only permitted wash is a 4% plum-to-canvas gradient behind the Today hero.

**Type.** Every face is on Google Fonts and in `@expo-google-fonts`. **Inter** (400/500/600, with tabular numerals turned on for all numbers) replaces Work Sans and handles all UI and data. **Instrument Serif Italic** is kept for exactly three uses: the user's quoted words, the one hero prompt on Today, and the summary title. There is no 700 weight anywhere in the body. Hanken Grotesk is an acceptable warmer alternative to Inter, and Source Serif 4 is the fallback if Instrument Serif's single weight feels too thin in quotes.

Keep `allowFontScaling` on and cap `maxFontSizeMultiplier` at about 1.6 only on chips and the tab bar. Test layouts at the largest accessibility size.

**Space, shape and cards.** The layout uses a 4 pt grid with a spacing scale of 4 · 8 · 12 · 16 · 20 · 24 · 32 · 40 · 56 and a 20 pt screen gutter. Radii are 20 for cards, 14 for inputs, 999 (pill) for chips and buttons, and 28 for the top of sheets. The current radius of 8 is part of why the app feels like a template. Cards are white on the canvas with a 1 px hairline border and **no shadow**. Only the Today hero and the document preview get a soft shadow (y 2, blur 16, `#1F1B18` at 6%). The one filled card allowed is plum-tint, and only for selection. Buttons are 56 pt primary (kept from the current `minTap` for fatigued hands), 48 pt secondary and 44 pt chips at minimum. Content is capped at 600 pt on tablets, as now.

**Iconography.** Use **Lucide** at one weight: a 1.75 pt stroke with rounded caps, at 20/24 pt. Icons are ink, or plum when interactive. There are no emoji, mascots, flowers, uteruses or moon phases, and no coloured icon circles per category. Entry-type glyphs are mic (voice note), pen (typed note), droplet (bleeding, the only clay icon), calendar (appointment) and pill (medicine).

**Charts.** Follow the rule "bars and heat-strips, not lines". The signature visual, used in-app on Timeline and on the PDF, is the **cycle-aligned heat-strip**: days run along the x-axis, logged bleeding days sit as a clay-tint band behind the strip, and each symptom has its own row of severity cells with the number inside the cell on the PDF. Frequency uses horizontal bars with a direct label and count ("Pelvic pain · 9 of 30 days"). Charts have no gridlines except a baseline, no legends where a direct label fits, no averages presented as scores, and no trend arrows. Every caption is descriptive only, for example "Pelvic pain was logged on 9 of 30 days; 7 of those were bleeding days." Never write "linked to", "triggered by" or "pattern suggests".

**Motion and haptics.** Use 200–280 ms ease-out fades and translates, and springs only where spatial continuity matters (the saved card settling into the For my doctor tab). There are no confetti, pulsing orbs or looping animations, and Reduce Motion replaces all motion with fades. Haptics use `expo-haptics`: `selectionAsync` on chip toggles and segmented controls, `impactAsync(Light)` on record start and stop, `notificationAsync(Success)` on save and when the PDF is created, and nothing on errors. No haptic pattern is documented by a leading app; these follow iOS convention.

### Tone and microcopy

The voice is Clue-plain, warmed by acknowledgement. Atossa speaks in the second person, briefly, acknowledges without evaluating, explains why it asks, always offers a way out, and never praises the act of logging. It keeps the user's own words in the record and uses plain labels (NHS style: "being sick", not "emesis"). It uses weight-neutral language throughout ([NHS writing guide](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/a-to-z-of-nhs-health-writing); [Teede et al.](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/108/10/2447/7242360)).

| Moment | Write | Never write |
|---|---|---|
| Opening prompt | "What's been going on with your body? Say it however it comes. Messy is fine." | "Describe your symptoms." / "Ask me anything!" |
| Acknowledge | "Got it." / "Thanks, that's noted." | "That sounds like…", "This is common with…", "That's normal." |
| Why we ask | "GPs usually ask this, so it helps to have it ready." | "This helps us assess your risk." |
| Follow-up | "Is it only around your period, at other times too, or all the time?" | "Is your pain cyclical?" |
| Confirmation | "Here's what I noted. Tap anything to fix it." | "Analysis complete." |
| Save | "Saved. 13 notes ready for your GP." | "Great job! 🔥 5-day streak!" |
| Empty Timeline | "Nothing here yet. Your notes will line up here by date." | "You haven't logged in 6 days." |
| Reminder | "If today's worth noting, I'm here. Two minutes is plenty." | "Don't break your streak!" / "You missed yesterday." |
| Summary intro | "This is what you told Atossa between 14 Jul and 29 Sep, in your words." | "Your symptom report indicates…" |
| Chart caption | "Pelvic pain: 9 of 30 days. 7 of them were bleeding days." | "Pain is linked to your period." |
| Out of scope | "I can't say what might be causing this. That's one to ask your GP, and I've put it on your list." | anything naming a condition, cause or next treatment |

Always show a fixed safety line in Profile and at the foot of the summary builder: "If you feel very unwell or you're worried, contact your GP, NHS 111 or 999." The line must be static. It must **never be triggered by what the user says**, because content-triggered alerts are a form of triage, which is diagnostic.

### Do not do

| Pattern | Why it's banned |
|---|---|
| Naming any condition, "match" scores, "possible causes", risk levels (Flo checker, Ada report) | Diagnostic; the product's core rule |
| Comparing values with guideline thresholds ("irregular", ">35 days", "heavy") or labels like "typical/atypical" (Clue analysis) | Implicit diagnosis; show raw values only |
| Cycle rings, "days until", fertile windows, striped "predicted" days, forecasts | Prediction; fails irregular cycles |
| "Linked to", "triggers", correlation grids, trend arrows, averages shown as scores | Implies cause without numbers |
| Suggesting treatments, remedies, supplements, "try…", or "see a doctor if…" triggered by content | Treatment advice and triage |
| Streaks, broken-chain calendars, red missed days, "you missed…" copy, confetti | Guilt; bad days are when data matters most |
| Paywalls, upsell pop-ups, a "gift" discount, commitment gestures | The top complaint about Flo, Clue and Stardust |
| Several decorative accent colours; colour that changes by tab; salmon or any pastel carrying text | No semantics; fails contrast (salmon 1.72:1) |
| Traffic-light severity; red/green on anything bodily; alarm gauges ("CONCERNING") | Reads as a judgement and causes anxiety |
| Bold-everything type; a serif for every heading | Nothing stands out; it clashes |
| Dense dashboards, chip walls, more than one hero per screen, 4-tile stat grids | Needs brain-fog capacity users lack |
| Full-screen voice orb with no visible transcript; press-and-hold recording | Hides text; hard for people in pain |
| Liquid Glass or translucency behind health content | Legibility risk |
| Stock photos of women, flowers, uteruses, moon phases, mascots | The pink cliché and false intimacy |
| Weight-centric or gendered copy | Guideline asks for weight-inclusive language; excludes some users |
| Multi-page raw-data PDFs as the default export | GPs don't read them |
| Claiming the app "speeds up diagnosis" | Unevidenced and diagnostic in framing |

### What is still uncertain

None of this has been tested with PCOS or endometriosis users. Most hex values in the market survey are estimates from screenshots, and the Oura tokens are reverse-engineered, so the palette above is original rather than copied. No study compares tap-to-record with hold-to-record, or shows that one-page summaries change GP behaviour. Whether `expo-glass-effect` is stable on SDK 57 is unverified. The EHP-5 wording is licensed, so impact questions must be paraphrased. The fastest validation would be to put the Today → capture → "Here's what I noted" → summary preview flow in front of five patients and two GPs, and time the capture against a 60-second target.

## Conclusion

The competitive gap is not visual polish. Every well-funded app in the set is polished. The gap is that none of them keeps the patient's own words and hands them to a clinician in the shape of a clinical history, and most weaken trust through prediction, paywalls or diagnostic-sounding scores. That makes Atossa's restrictions its design brief. No prediction removes the ring and frees the home screen for the voice button. No interpretation turns confirmation into a mirror of the user's words. The GP audience turns the summary into a one-page document built around NICE's history items, not a dashboard.

For the rebuild, two things matter more than the palette. First, the **quote-plus-tidy-value row** is the product's atomic unit. The same component should appear in capture confirmation, in the Timeline and on the PDF, so the user's words visibly travel from the moment they speak to the page the GP reads. Second, the no-diagnosis rule has to be enforced in the design system itself and not only in the model prompt. That means no threshold labels, no causal captions, no content-triggered alerts, and a server-side filter on any generated text. The visual direction can change later. That enforcement is what makes Atossa a trustworthy clinical notebook rather than another symptom checker.
